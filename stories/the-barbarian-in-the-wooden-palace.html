The Barbarian in the Wooden Palace

Attila never conquered Rome — he charged it rent instead, ran the operation out of a wooden palace, and drank from a wooden cup while his guests drank from gold

Samdapau · 7 min read
Text size

In the autumn of 449, a Roman embassy rode into the headquarters of the most feared man on earth, and found him drinking out of a wooden cup.

The visitors had come a long way to be unnerved. They were diplomats from Constantinople, on their way to the court of Attila, king of the Huns, whose encampment stood somewhere on the Hungarian plain — a village of wooden houses that functioned as the capital of an empire stretching from Central Europe deep into the steppe. One of them, a secretary named Priscus, wrote down what he saw, and his account survives in fragments. It is the best seat we have at that table, and the table is the whole story.

At the banquet, the guests lounged on couches and were served course after course on silver plates, drinking from goblets of gold. Attila sat in the middle on a plain couch. His plate was a wooden trencher. His cup was wood. His clothes were clean and completely without ornament — no jewels on his sword belt, no gold on his boots, though the men around him glittered. Singers performed songs about his victories. A jester capered and got the whole hall laughing — everyone except Attila, who sat motionless, unsmiling, until his youngest son climbed up beside him, and the king's face finally softened into a smile and a hand on the boy's cheek.

This was the terror of two empires. And the terror, on the best eyewitness evidence we have, was a man in plain clothes running a very unusual business out of a wooden palace.

The protection business

Strip away the legend and Attila's operation was simple, and embarrassingly modern. Rome — both halves of it, eastern and western — paid him not to visit.

The payments were called tribute, subsidy, gifts between friends, anything but what they were. The eastern empire's chroniclers record the going rate: after the treaty of Margus in 435, seven hundred pounds of gold a year; after Attila's armies had twice demonstrated the alternative in the Balkans, the price rose — by the treaty of 447 the arrears alone were set at six thousand pounds of gold, with twenty-one hundred a year thereafter. Medieval chroniclers were casual with numbers, so treat those as invoices from a frightened customer's paperwork rather than audited accounts. But the direction of travel is not in doubt. Year after year, wagons of Roman gold rolled north to the wooden palace, and Attila distributed it to his followers, whose loyalty was the only real asset his confederation had.

A protection enterprise has one delicate problem: you must never actually destroy the customer. Attila understood this in his bones. He raided to renegotiate, not to bankrupt. Constantinople's walls he never seriously attempted, and Rome itself he never reached. The threat was the product; the gold was the revenue; the army was, in effect, his sales department.

He had not founded the firm, either. The Hunnic kingship came to him in 434 as a family inheritance, shared with his elder brother Bleda — two chief executives for one confederation. In 445 Bleda died, and the sources, being too polite or too frightened to say it plainly, leave the strong impression that Attila's hand was in it. From then on there was one signature on the account. Rome, which preferred negotiating with families it could divide, now had to deal with a sole proprietor.

The business also ran on Roman talent. His secretaries were captured or defected Romans. His negotiators were Romans and Goths and whoever else was useful. One of his chief secretaries, a man named Orestes, was a Roman from Pannonia — whose son, in one of history's better punchlines, would grow up to be Romulus Augustulus, the last emperor of the West. Priscus's embassy, meanwhile, had a second purpose besides diplomacy: it was carrying an assassination plot, hatched in the palace at Constantinople, to murder Attila using his own envoy as the middleman. Attila knew. He knew almost immediately. And his response was a masterpiece of customer relations — he sent the would-be assassin's handler back home carrying the bribe money in a bag, with a message for the emperor that amounted to: you should be ashamed of yourself, and no, the subscription is not cancelled.

The year he went west

The standing quarrel of those years shows you how the whole arrangement worked. Every treaty required Rome to hand back Hunnic fugitives — deserters, runaway princes, inconvenient cousins sheltering in Roman territory — and Rome kept stalling, because sheltering a customer's rivals is a classic way to keep the price down. Attila treated each delayed extradition the way a landlord treats a late cheque: as a test of whether the agreement still held. Embassies shuttled back and forth over lists of names while the gold, mostly, kept moving. It was diplomacy conducted as debt collection, and both sides understood the paperwork perfectly.

In 450 the western market tried to change the terms. The emperor's sister, Justa Grata Honoria, trapped in a forced engagement she did not want, sent Attila her ring — so the sources tell it — asking him to rescue her. Attila, who was nobody's fool, chose to read the ring as a marriage proposal, claimed Honoria as his bride, and demanded half the western empire as her dowry. It was the largest invoice ever presented to Rome, and he presented it with a straight face.

When the West refused to pay, he finally invaded — Gaul, in 451, with a confederation army the chroniclers swell to numbers nobody should repeat with a straight face either. And there, near Châlons, he met the one Roman general who knew him personally. Flavius Aetius, the West's strongman, had spent part of his youth as a hostage in the Hunnic camp and knew exactly what he was facing. Aetius assembled Romans, Visigoths, Franks and Alans into the least enthusiastic coalition in Roman history, and at the Catalaunian Plains the two armies fought each other to a standstill in one of the bloodiest days of the fifth century. Attila withdrew in good order, wagon-fort and all. It was the one time the protection business met a customer willing to fight — and neither side cared to repeat the experiment.

The next year he came down into Italy instead. Aquileia he destroyed. Milan and Padua he took. And then — he stopped, and went home. Rome loved to say that Pope Leo met him on the road and talked him out of it with sheer holiness; the meeting may even have happened. But his army was starving in a famine year, disease was in the camps, the eastern empire was raiding his now-unguarded homelands, and the new eastern emperor had simply cancelled the subscription. Attila went home to renegotiate the only way he knew: by preparing a bigger army.

The wedding night

He never got to use it. In 453, back in the wooden palace, Attila married again — a girl named Ildico — and celebrated, Priscus says, with excessive drinking. In the night, lying on his back in a stupor, he suffered a nosebleed; the blood ran down his throat instead of out, and the most feared man in the world suffocated quietly in his sleep, without a single enemy in the room. His warriors found him in the morning, cut off their own hair and gashed their faces in mourning — the one moment of gore in the whole record, and it was grief.

For the burial, the later writer Jordanes tells the famous version: the body laid in a triple coffin of gold, silver and iron; the grave dug in secret; a river diverted over it; the grave-diggers killed to keep the secret. Read that as the legend already at work within a generation. What is certain is the aftermath. Within a year his sons were at war with each other over the inheritance; in 454 the subject peoples rose and broke the confederation at the river Nedao; and the enterprise — which had never been an empire so much as one man's network of threats, marriages, hostages and gold — dissolved, because there was no institution left when the founder's heart stopped. The gold stopped flowing in, so the loyalty evaporated. It had always been a business with exactly one employee who mattered.

Only later did church writers brand him flagellum Dei, the Scourge of God, as if he had been a natural disaster sent to punish Rome. Priscus, who sat at his table, would not have recognised the demon. He had seen a plain-dressed executive in a wooden office, drinking from a wooden cup, ruling half the world with the most reliable product of the fifth century: a threat, renewed annually.

He never conquered Rome. He did something cleverer — he made Rome pay him rent for its own survival.

Which is the stronger empire — the one that wins the war, or the one that gets paid every year without fighting it?
