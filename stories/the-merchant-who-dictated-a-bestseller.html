The Merchant Who Dictated a Bestseller

Seventeen years on the Khan's staff, one sea voyage home, and a memoir co-written with a romance novelist in a Genoese prison

Samdapau · 8 min read
Text size

A prison in Genoa, in 1298, was not the obvious place to produce one of the best-selling books of the Middle Ages. But wars are good for publishing. Venice and Genoa had fought at sea off Curzola that September, and among the Venetian prisoners mouldering in Genoese cells were two men with complementary problems.

One was Rustichello of Pisa — a professional writer of Arthurian romances, a man who knew exactly how a bestselling book was built and had no material. The other was a forty-four-year-old Venetian merchant named Marco Polo, who had material beyond the dreams of any romance-writer alive and no way to set it down. He had been home from the East for barely three years. The tales he told at dinner made his fellow Venetians shake their heads.

So the merchant dictated, and the romance-writer wrote. It may be the most productive prison collaboration in history — a family firm's travelling salesman and a professional storyteller, locked in a cell together, turning twenty-four years of Asia into a book.

The family firm sends an intern

The Polos were not explorers. They were import-export. Marco's father Niccolò and his uncle Maffeo were Venetian merchants with a house in Constantinople and a sideline in the long, ruinous, profitable roads east. On their first journey, in the 1260s, they had reached the court of Kublai Khan, the Mongol ruler of China, who received them with interest — and sent them back west as his couriers, carrying a request to the Pope: send me oil from the lamp at the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem, and a hundred Christian scholars to teach my court.

The firm attempted delivery. In 1271 the brothers set out again — and this time they took seventeen-year-old Marco along. Think of him as the new intern on the family account: cheap, expendable, and there to learn the trade in the place the trade actually happened. The two learned friars the Pope assigned to the mission looked at the war-torn road ahead and quit within the year. The oil, the letters, and the boy went on without them. The journey out took three and a half years — through Persia, over the Pamir passes where the party lost a year to Marco's illness, down into China. In 1275 the Polos were presented at Kublai's summer palace at Shangdu, the place the poets would call Xanadu. The hundred scholars had shrunk to one teenage apprentice. The Khan, by the book's account, took a shine to him.

Seventeen years on the Khan's staff

What Marco became, he describes himself: a foreign staffer in the largest administration on earth. He says he learned four languages and their scripts. And he found his niche in the way observant employees always do — by noticing what the boss actually wanted. Kublai sent officials to the far provinces and was bored rigid by their reports: taxes, tolls, nothing else. Marco, sent on missions of his own, came back with the taxes and the strange customs, the odd animals, the story of the place. The Khan, the book says, listened to him at length, and sent him on the missions nobody else could narrate. It is the oldest career advice in the world: the reporting is the job.

What he reported is the book: the cities of China with populations Europeans could not credit, the courier network with its thousands of relay stations, the coal that burns like stone, and above all the thing his listeners back home could not swallow at all — the Khan's money, which was made of mulberry bark:

He makes his money after this fashion. He causes the bark of the mulberry tree to be stripped away and made into something like paper, and this passes current as money all over his country.
— paraphrased from The Book of Ser Marco Polo, in Henry Yule's translation (1871)

Some of what he claimed for himself, readers then and since have squinted at — a governorship of the great city of Yangzhou, for instance, which Chinese records do not confirm. He was a favoured foreign employee with a merchant's eye and a traveller's memory, dictating years later; the embroidery, where there is embroidery, is exactly where you would expect it: around his own importance. It is rarely around the prices.

The delivery run home

In 1292 came the assignment that took him home. A Mongol princess, Kököchin, was to be married to the Khan's nephew, the ruler of Persia, and the Polos — arguing that no one alive knew the sea road better — talked their way into the escort fleet, with leave to go home afterwards. Fourteen great ships, two years of monsoons and disease; of the hundreds who sailed, only a handful of envoys and the princess arrived. They delivered her to Persia and found her bridegroom dead, so she married his son instead. Then the Polos kept going: Constantinople, and finally, in 1295, Venice.

Venetians later loved a homecoming scene — the ragged travellers unrecognised at their own door, cutting open their coats to spill out the jewels sewn inside. It is a lovely story, and it comes from a much later edition of the tale, added centuries on; the original book does not have it. The book doesn't need it. Keep the detail in mind, though, because it matters for the argument below: the legend embroidered Polo freely, which is precisely why later readers began to wonder how much of the cloth was his.

The book, and the doubters

The prison dictation, written in the Franco-Italian of Rustichello's trade, called itself a description of the world — Le Devisement du Monde. Venetians nicknamed it Il Milione, "the Million," usually explained by Marco's habit of counting the Khan's revenues in millions and his fellow citizens' disbelief. No original manuscript survives. What survives is a family of copies — well over a hundred of them, in half a dozen languages, no two quite identical, because for a century and a half every copy was written out by hand and every copyist trimmed, padded or translated as he pleased. The book we argue about today is a crowd of related texts, not a single one — which means the argument about what Marco Polo actually said is, in part, permanently unresolvable. It was copied, abridged, translated, argued over, and used — used most of all by other merchants and map-makers, who treated it as what it was: a commercial intelligence report from inside an empire, written by a man who noticed roads, taxes, salt revenues and sailing seasons. Christopher Columbus owned a copy and filled its margins with notes; the book that nobody quite believed sent a later sailor west looking for its harbours.

In 1995 the question was put point-blank in a famous study — Did Marco Polo Go to China? — and the omissions were lined up like evidence. He never mentions the Great Wall. He never mentions tea. He never mentions footbinding, or Chinese writing, though he must have seen all of them. His names for places are Persian and Mongol, not Chinese. He was in the Khan's service for seventeen years and never learned to say so in Chinese ink.

The majority of specialists today answer that he went — and their case is built not on his wonders but on his paperwork. The Great Wall that tourists photograph is mostly Ming-dynasty work, built centuries after him; in his day much of it was ruined rammed earth a traveller could cross without noticing a monument. The omissions cut, but the inclusions convict: his figures for the salt tax, his account of the paper money's denominations and manufacture, match Chinese records in detail no European library of his age could have supplied him. And the princess convoy — the trip home — appears in both Chinese and Persian records with the same mission and near-identical names for its three envoys. A stay-at-home compiler could have stolen marvels. The boring, checkable, correct details are much harder to fake.

Which leaves the honest verdict, and it doubles as the man's epitaph. There is a deathbed line the tradition hands him — friends begging him to retract his tall tales, Polo replying that he had not told half of what he saw. It appears long after his death and cannot be verified; it survives because it sounds like him. What can be verified is stranger than the line: a teenage intern from a Venetian import firm spent seventeen years inside the largest empire on earth, came home, fell into the right prison with the right ghost-writer — and produced the report that taught Europe what Asia was.

The wonders made him famous. The paperwork made him believable.

When you bring home a story no one can check, which do you lead with — the marvels, or the receipts?
