The Monk Who Posted a Notice

An internal academic memo, a new machine called print, and the friar who lost control of his own document

Samdapau · 8 min read
Text size

Every schoolbook has the picture: a monk in a brown habit, hammer in hand, nailing a long list of complaints to a church door while the town of Wittenberg gathers round. It is the most famous memo in European history — the day in October 1517 when one man took on the largest institution in the world.

Here is the problem. The hammer is probably fiction. The scene was first described decades later by Luther's younger colleague Melanchthon, who was not in Wittenberg in 1517, and modern scholars have doubted the nails-and-door drama ever since the question was first raised. The church door did serve as the university's notice board, so something may have been pinned there. But what Luther demonstrably did that autumn was far more modern, and far harder to undo.

He mailed it.

The memo was never meant for you

Martin Luther was not a rebel with a printing strategy. He was a thirty-three-year-old Augustinian friar, and before that he had been something else entirely: a promising law student, the son of a self-made mine manager who had bought his boy the education that would carry the family into the professional class. By Luther's own later telling, the law career ended in a thunderstorm in July 1505, when a lightning bolt near the village of Stotternheim threw the terrified student to the ground and he cried out to St. Anne for his life, vowing to become a monk. His father was furious — years of investment, gone into a friary — but the vow held. That is the man who filed the memo: not a born revolutionary but a company man of two institutions, the monastery and the university, who had already once ruined a family's plans for the sake of a terrified conscience.

By 1517 he had two jobs: he lectured on the Bible at the little university of Wittenberg, and he heard confessions in the town church. The two jobs collided in the confessional.

The collision arrived in the form of paperwork. A Dominican friar named Johann Tetzel was touring the territories near Saxony selling indulgences — certificates, issued under papal authority, that remitted the penalty for sins in purgatory. The campaign was raising money to rebuild St. Peter's in Rome, and, less advertised, to service the enormous banking debts of the young Archbishop Albrecht of Mainz. Tetzel was barred from Electoral Saxony itself — Luther's prince kept the rival show out — but Wittenbergers could simply walk across the border to buy. And they did. Then they came home, Luther later wrote with fury, and waved the certificates in confession: why repent? The account was settled. The paper said so. A jingle followed Tetzel's tour, usually attributed to him: the moment the coin rings in the coffer, the soul springs out of purgatory. Whether Tetzel coined it or his critics did, it was the slogan his customers believed.

Luther's objections were theological, not theatrical, and he was a lecturer. So he did what lecturers do. He drafted a set of ninety-five short propositions in Latin — the Disputation on the Power and Efficacy of Indulgences — the standard opening move for an academic debate nobody was expected to attend beyond the faculty. And he sent a courteously worded copy to Archbishop Albrecht himself, on 31 October 1517, with a deferential letter asking the authorities to rein in the sellers. The letter survives. It is polite to the point of discomfort.

That was the whole plan: an internal dispute memo, filed through channels, in the professionals' language, by a man whose occupation was teaching students and tending souls. Think of it as a complaint sent to head office with documentation attached.

The machine he didn't build

What happened next had nothing to do with Luther and everything to do with a technology he had not designed and could not steer: the printing press.

Somebody — Luther never worked out who, and his own surprise is all over his letters — translated the Latin theses into German. Printers in Nuremberg, Leipzig and Basel had editions out within weeks. By the start of 1518 the internal memo was being read in taverns and market towns across the German lands, by people who could not have found Wittenberg on a map. The reason was economics, not theology. Print shops were businesses; Luther sold. In the decade that followed he became the first true bestseller of the print age, his pamphlets and sermons pouring from the presses in such volume that by some counts, in the peak years of the 1520s, a remarkable share of everything printed in German came from his pen.

Picture the position he was in. An author today can watch a post escape and delete it, clarify it, apologise for it. Luther had no version of any of that. There was no master copy he controlled, no recall, no edit. Reprints bred reprints, each generation copied from the last, and the memo he had addressed to a handful of Latin-reading colleagues now belonged to a continent of readers he had never intended to address — many of them reading conclusions into it that horrified the cautious lecturer who wrote it. He had filed a document. The document had gone into distribution.

You cannot unpublish a continent

Nor did the author stay quiet while his memo escaped. Freed from any hope that head office would fix things quietly, Luther went all in on the distribution channel he had accidentally discovered. In a single year, 1520, he published the three works that turned a disciplinary quarrel into a movement: an open letter to the German nobility calling on lay rulers to reform a church the clergy would not; a treatise arguing that Rome held the sacraments captive; and a short, luminous essay on Christian freedom. The memo-writer had become a publishing phenomenon feeding the very presses that made him unsuppressible — pamphlets of a few sheets, cheap, fast, written in German a market-town reader could follow. The internal dispute was now a backlist.

Rome, moving at institutional speed, eventually caught up with the print run. There were interrogations, a disputation at Leipzig in 1519 where his opponent pushed him into denying that church councils could err, and in 1520 a papal bull threatening excommunication. Luther, the courteous memo-writer of 1517, publicly burned the bull that December. The channels had failed; the document was bigger than both of them now.

In April 1521 he was summoned to the imperial Diet at Worms to answer for the stack of books laid out on a table before the young Emperor Charles V. Recant them, he was told. He asked for a day to think — the frightened friar of the legend is in the record; he prayed through the night. The next evening he refused. The official protocol records the substance: his conscience was captive to the Word of God, he could not recant anything without being shown his error, and so he could do no other. The sentence that rings down the centuries — Here I stand — appears in the printed version of the speech that circulated afterwards, not in the official minutes. History, once again, was improved by the printers. But the stand itself, the refusal itself, is documented fact: the internal-memo author, in person, declining to withdraw the document.

The Edict of Worms made him an outlaw, which should have been the end of him. Instead his prince staged a kidnapping on the road home and hid him in the Wartburg castle under a false name, where the would-be nobody of Wittenberg spent ten months doing the one thing that guaranteed the memo could never be stamped out: he translated the New Testament into German, finishing the draft in about eleven weeks. The September Testament of 1522 was another print phenomenon. The channel-filed complaint had become, in five years, a national literature.

He never asked for any of the rest of it. When people began calling themselves Lutherans, he objected in print — let them not use his name, he wrote, in substance; he had not died for anybody. But an author can no more control a movement than recall a print run, and he knew it by then. He had learned what the presses of Germany taught everyone after him: publication is not a conversation you lead. It is a thing you release.

He wrote an internal memo for twenty colleagues, and the new machine delivered it to a continent; you cannot recall a document the presses have adopted.

What have you ever sent to a small room — a letter, a memo, a post — that the network would have forwarded to everyone, whether you meant it or not?
