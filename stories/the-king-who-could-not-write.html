The King Who Could Not Write

Charlemagne ran the largest empire in Europe by ear — voice, memory and other men's pens — and spent his nights under the pillow, losing a private war against the alphabet

Samdapau · 7 min read
Text size

The most powerful man in Europe was doing homework in bed.

It was late in his reign, and the empire was more or less run — an empire he had built himself, stretching from the mountains of Spain to the rivers of Germany, from the North Sea down into Italy. Kings answered to him. The Pope needed him. And at night, when the court at Aachen finally quieted down, the emperor kept wax tablets and blank sheets tucked under his pillow, and in his spare moments he would pull them out and practise.

Letters. His own name, laboriously, like a schoolboy. The hand of a lifelong soldier, trying to make it behave like the hand of a clerk.

His friend and courtier Einhard, who wrote his life, records the result with the tact of a man delivering bad news about someone he loved: the lessons began too late, and they never really took. Charles the Great — Charlemagne — conquered half of Europe and lost a long, quiet war against the alphabet.

Running an empire by ear

This needs explaining, because in Charlemagne's world it was not shameful. It was normal — almost.

Writing, in the eighth century, was a trade, like masonry. Kings did not do trades. What a king did was decide, out loud, in front of witnesses, and a room full of trained men turned the deciding into documents. Charlemagne ran the largest organisation in Europe the way a modern chief executive runs a company with a superb front office: everything arrived by ear and left by voice. Letters were read aloud to him. Reports were read aloud to him. At meals, Einhard tells us, readers performed for him — histories, and Augustine's City of God, a thousand-page theological brick, as dinner entertainment. His memory was the filing system. His voice was the signature.

And the voice worked in several languages. He spoke his native Frankish, handled Latin fluently, and understood Greek better than he could speak it. He studied grammar with an elderly deacon from Pisa, and astronomy and arithmetic with Alcuin of York, the most learned man he could hire — which, given that he could hire anybody, tells you something about the curriculum. He even had opinions about the calendar: Einhard lists the new Frankish names he invented for the months, renaming the year the way a founder renames the product line.

To keep the machine honest he ran an audit department. Twice a year, roughly, pairs of royal inspectors — the missi dominici, the lord's envoys, usually a bishop and a count travelling together — rode out into the provinces with a list of questions: Are the counts judging fairly? Are the taxes right? Is anyone oppressing widows this season? They heard complaints from anybody, reported back to the one man at the centre, and the centre acted on what it heard. It was management by walking about, except the walking was done by deputies and the listening was done by a king who could not take his own notes.

The great charters that survive carry his mark — an elaborate monogram, a diamond built out of the letters of his name, drawn up by his clerks. His hand is not on it. It never is.

An inheritance with a brother in it

He did not start as sole owner of the family business. When his father died in 768, the Frankish kingdom was split between Charles and his younger brother Carloman — two kings, one realm, mutual dislike included at no extra charge. For three years Europe held its breath. Then, in 771, Carloman died, suddenly and conveniently, and Charles took the whole kingdom. Carloman's widow fled to the Lombards in Italy. Charles followed the argument the way he followed most arguments: with an army. By 774 he wore the Lombard crown too.

Then came the Saxons, on his north-eastern border, and that war ground on for thirty years. By the end of the century he ruled most of western and central Europe — the largest territory the West had seen held together since Rome fell, three hundred years before. A company assembled, deal by deal and war by war, by a man who conducted every negotiation face to face, because face to face was the only interface he fully trusted.

Christmas morning in Rome

Which brings us to the strangest board meeting of the Middle Ages.

In the year 800, Charles was in Rome, sorting out the affairs of Pope Leo III — a pope who had been attacked in the street by his enemies and had fled to Charles for protection. Charles restored him. Charles protected him. Charles was, at that moment, the most useful friend the papacy had ever had.

On Christmas Day, in St Peter's, as Charles rose from prayer before the altar, Leo stepped forward with a crown and put it on his head, and the congregation shouted him Emperor of the Romans.

Publicly: a coronation. Privately: a takeover of the narrative. The title had not been Charles's idea — at least, that is what he afterwards insisted, and Einhard records the claim in dead earnest:

Einhard says the new emperor declared that he would not have entered the church that day at all, great feast though it was, if he had known what the Pope intended to do.

Believe the politeness or not, the politics are easy to read. Leo had handed Charles a crown — and in the same gesture, made the papacy the hand that crowns. The empire's biggest stakeholder had just acquired him, at a discount, in public, on Christmas morning. Charles spent years afterwards managing that relationship, and in 813 he crowned his own son himself, in his own chapel at Aachen, no pope invited. A man learns.

The infrastructure project

Here is the part of the story that outlasted the wars.

Charles looked at his empire and noticed that the whole machine ran on paper nobody could write and Latin nobody could spell. Priests misread their own service books. Counts could not produce a competent clerk between them. So in 789 he issued a general order — schools at every monastery and cathedral, teaching reading, singing, grammar and reckoning — and at his own court he built the prototype: the palace school, staffed with imported talent, Alcuin at its head.

His own children, daughters included, were put through the liberal arts like junior staff through a training programme. Out of his scribes' push for documents that could actually be read came a clean, standard handwriting — the clear letterforms that spread across Europe and that your printed letters still, faintly, descend from. He could not write, so he fixed writing: standardised it, staffed it, funded it, and ordered it taught in every province he owned. It is the oldest trick in management. The boss's personal weakness became the company's biggest infrastructure investment.

The tablets under the pillow

And still, at night, the tablets.

Understand what they mean. By then there was no examination left for him to pass, no superior to impress, no practical need at all — his empire had hundreds of men who could write beautifully for him. The practising was private, late, and by Einhard's honest account, unsuccessful:

He also tried to write, and used to keep tablets and blanks in bed under his pillow, that at leisure hours he might accustom his hand to form the letters; however, as he did not begin his efforts in due season, but late in life, they met with ill success.
— Einhard, The Life of Charlemagne, in Samuel Epes Turner's translation (1880)

Modern scholars argue about how far his reading went; the sources are a courtier's compliments, not a school report. But the tablets are not in dispute, and neither is the order of events: the skill he failed to learn was the one he made compulsory for an empire.

He died at Aachen in 814. The empire passed whole to his one surviving son, and within a generation his grandsons had cut it into pieces at the negotiating table — the usual fate of a founder's life work once the founder leaves the room. The schools, the handwriting, the habit of expecting kings to be educated: those survived the partition. They are, in the end, the larger empire.

He never learned to write his name. He taught a continent to write its own.

What is the one basic skill you would still sit up in bed practising, at the top of the world, with nobody left to grade you?
