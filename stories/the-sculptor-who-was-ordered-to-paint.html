The Sculptor Who Was Ordered to Paint

Europe's greatest sculptor kept being handed a painter's jobs by the most demanding client alive — and the wrong jobs made him immortal

Samdapau · 7 min read
Text size

In the spring of 1508, the most powerful man in Europe summoned the greatest sculptor alive and gave him an order he had been dreading: put down the chisel, climb a scaffold, and paint a ceiling.

The sculptor was Michelangelo, thirty-three years old, and he was in the middle of the commission of the century — a colossal marble tomb for this same pope, forty statues, years of work already spent quarrying the stone. Painting, he protested, was not his art. He was so sure the assignment was a trap — that his rivals at court had talked the pope into it, hoping the stone-carver would fall off his scaffold in public disgrace — that he said so to the man's face and suggested a younger painter, Raphael, for the job instead.

The pope was Julius II, and Julius did not take suggestions. Michelangelo would paint the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel. He would paint it in fresco, on wet plaster, overhead, a technique he had barely touched since boyhood. It took him four years, wrecked his eyesight and his back, and produced the most famous painting on Earth.

He had been right, though. It was never his job. That was rather the story of his life.

Trained on stone

The irony begins in the workshop. At thirteen, Michelangelo was apprenticed to Domenico Ghirlandaio — a painter — and lasted about a year before gravitating to the Medici sculpture garden, where an old sculptor taught him the ancient art of carving marble. From then on his identity was fixed, and he defended it like a surname. In Florence he carved the David between 1501 and 1504, seventeen feet of marble giant-killer — and carved it, moreover, out of a block other sculptors had already spoiled: a giant slab quarried forty years earlier, begun and botched by another hand, and left standing in the cathedral works yard as a warning. Florence offered it around; the young Michelangelo asked for the ruined stone, and pulled the most perfect statue of the Renaissance out of it. By his late twenties he was the acknowledged master of the hardest, least forgiving art there is. Stone does not forgive. A fresco can be scraped off and redone; a chisel stroke too deep is forever.

Through sixty years of fame, through commissions in painting, architecture, military engineering and city planning, he signed his letters the same way: Michelangelo, sculptor. It was not false modesty. It was a craftsman stating his trade — the way a carpenter, handed a violin to repair, might keep pointing at his toolbox.

The client from hell

We should talk about the client. Julius II was a warrior pope who led armies in person, bullied kings, and had decided to rebuild Rome — and St Peter's — at a scale not seen since the Caesars. For a man like that, artists were not contractors. They were staff. In 1505 he summoned Michelangelo and commissioned his own tomb: a freestanding mountain of marble, forty statues, to be placed in the new St Peter's. Michelangelo spent eight months in the quarries at Carrara choosing the blocks, and came back to Rome glowing.

Then the pope's attention wandered to the bigger project. The money followed it. When Michelangelo arrived for payment and instructions, he was — by the account of his friend and biographer Condivi — turned away from the pope's door by a servant, more than once. In April 1506 he packed, fled Rome the day before Julius was due to return, and refused, from Florentine soil, to come back. It took the political pressure of two governments to drag him to a reconciliation in Bologna, where Julius, mollified, promptly commissioned a colossal bronze statue of himself. (The Bolognese tore it down and melted it into a cannon three years later. Michelangelo's clients were not the only difficult people in Italy.)

The tomb, though — the tomb became what he himself called the tragedy of his life. It was renegotiated, shrunk and resurrected across forty years and a succession of contracts, until the mountain of forty statues stood finally erected in 1545 as a wall monument with a handful of figures, chief among them the great Moses, carved decades earlier for a grander design. Every client since who has halved the budget, moved the deadline and kept the ambition should recognise the paperwork.

The ceiling he wasn't qualified for

So to 1508, and the pope turning his frustrated tomb-builder loose on the Sistine ceiling. Condivi reports Michelangelo's belief that Bramante, architect of the new St Peter's and no friend, had promoted the idea precisely because fresco was not his trade and failure was likely. If so, it is the worst sabotage in art history. The original plan, by Michelangelo's own later account, was modest — twelve apostles around the edge of the vault — and it was he who talked the pope out of it, telling him it would turn out a poor thing, and got permission instead to fill the whole ceiling with the history of the world before the Flood. Even sabotaged, the man could not resist enlarging the job. Then he dismissed the fresco assistants he had summoned from Florence, locked himself in the chapel, and taught himself the medium alone, at altitude, standing on a scaffold of his own design with his head thrown back — painters in later films lie him flat on his back like a patient, but the poem and the physics say he stood, and suffered standing.

His own verdict on the working conditions survives, in a comic sonnet he sent a friend mid-job — a masterpiece of self-pity from a man standing on a scaffold with his face tilted to the ceiling: beard pointing at heaven, skull resting on his shoulders, brush forever dripping paint onto his face. The poem's tail lands the punchline he repeated all his life: I am not a painter.

Vasari tells of the day Julius came in person, demanding to know when it would all be finished, and got the answer "when I can" — whereupon the pope, in a flash of the famous temper, threatened to have the painter thrown down off his own scaffold. The story may be polished, but the dynamic is corroborated by every contract the two men ever signed: an employee who could not be replaced, working for an employer who could not be refused.

The ceiling was unveiled in 1512. Four years, three hundred figures, one ruined posture — and the supposed non-painter had reset the standard of Western painting in a single vault. Julius died months later. The next pope was a Medici, and the assignments simply changed letterhead.

Sixty years of scope creep

For the rest of his life, pope after pope treated the finest sculptor in Europe as a general-purpose genius to be pointed at whatever needed doing. Leo X ordered a marble facade for San Lorenzo in Florence — three years of quarrying and road-building in the mountains, then the contract was cancelled. Clement VII gave him the Medici Chapel (sculpture, at last) but also the Laurentian Library, and in 1529 the defence of Florence itself, whereupon the Republic's master sculptor found himself designing fortifications against an imperial siege. Paul III summoned the sixty-one-year-old to paint again — the Last Judgment, another wall, another fresco, another masterpiece he had not applied for — and in 1546 put him in charge of St Peter's dome. For that job, Vasari tells us, he refused a salary: it was for the love of God. He redesigned the dome, had a great wooden model of it built so the work could continue after him, and was still directing the construction eighteen years later, the longest-serving unpaid architect in the Church's history — the sculptor, on his final assignment, doing a third profession's masterpiece as a volunteer.

He died in Rome in February 1564, a few weeks short of eighty-nine, still grumbling, still working. The David, the tomb that never happened, the chapel ceilings, the dome of St Peter's — the body of work of a man who kept being assigned the wrong job by clients too powerful to refuse.

Here is the lesson the centuries have kept. We call the ceiling his triumph. He called it an interruption. The two greatest paintings of the Renaissance exist because the wrong man was ordered onto a scaffold and was too proud — and too afraid of disgrace — to be anything less than Michelangelo up there.

He spent a lifetime insisting he was the wrong man for the job — and the jobs became the proof that he was the only man for them.

When the most powerful client of your life hands you the wrong job, do you refuse — or do you make it the masterpiece?
